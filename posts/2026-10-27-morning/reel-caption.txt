Court papers unsealed Monday show the Justice Department charged James Comey on October 5 with leaking national defense information to the New York Times in 2016. No grand jury voted; a prosecutor filed it directly. Two earlier investigations charged no one. Comey, charged twice before in a year, says he is still innocent.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 6: The government says a man named James Comey, who used to run the FBI, told a newspaper a secret ten years ago. They have tried to take him to court three times now, like knocking on the same door three times. He says he did nothing wrong and isn't afraid. A judge will look, and the grown-ups have it.

At 11: James Comey ran the FBI in 2016. The government says he told the New York Times a secret. Two teams of prosecutors looked and charged nobody. Ten years on, the DOJ charged him anyway and skipped the grand jury, the citizens' panel that checks evidence first. A referee skipping the replay.

At 17: Why charge a man now for what two investigations cleared ten years ago? The DOJ says it filed to stop a ten-year clock, a routine move. His lawyers say no judge or grand jury approved it, a week before the midterms. Second: it's his third charge in a year. When does prosecution become punishment?

Why this works at age 11: This age tests whether adults apply the rules evenly, so the script runs the chain: a secret in 2016, two checks, no charges, then a charge on the last day with the citizens' panel skipped. The referee is the mechanism. Expect "so is he guilty?" Nobody knows. A charge is not a verdict, and the first case was thrown out.

The dinner table question: If two referees said no foul, should a third get to call one? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
