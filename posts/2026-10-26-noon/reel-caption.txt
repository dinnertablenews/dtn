Brazil's president took 51.79% in Sunday's runoff, 4.4 million votes clear of Flávio Bolsonaro, three weeks after losing the first round. Nobody had done that since civilian rule returned in 1985. At age 6: Brazil picked its president on Sunday, and Lula won. He's 80, and he lost the first vote three weeks ago, so most people thought he was out. Then far more people turned up to vote, and he won by 4.4 million votes, enough to fill about 50 football stadiums. The other man is cross about it, but the count is finished.

At 9: Bolsonaro beat Lula in round one on 4 October, 47% to 45%. But 33 million Brazilians skipped it, so Lula's team spent three weeks getting them to the polls. On Sunday far more turned out, and Lula won 51.79% to 48.21%. Round one was the heat. Only the final counts.

At 13: Can the loser just refuse the result? Bolsonaro calls Sunday's vote "impossible": a judge, once Lula's lawyer, kept polls open two extra hours. But both camps asked, and the court's president, named by Bolsonaro's father, says it changed nothing. Who rules on a close election?

Why this works at age 13: A teenager can hold two true things at once: the judge who extended voting was once Lula's lawyer, and Bolsonaro's own camp asked for the extra hours. The script asks who rules on fairness. In the articles it is a court president the other side appointed. Expect "could it still be overturned?" Lawyers told the BBC the chance is remote.
