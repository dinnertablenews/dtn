At age 7: In a country far away called Myanmar, soldiers hurt a group of people called the Rohingya. Most of them ran to the next country over, the way you'd run to a neighbour's house. On Thursday the world's top judges said out loud that it was wrong. You are safe here, and the grown-ups have it.

At 8: In 2017 Myanmar's army attacked the Rohingya, a Muslim minority. The judges found about 10,000 killed and 700,000 driven into Bangladesh. They voted 9 to 6: genocide, a first for any country. The court is a referee with a whistle and no handcuffs. It can call the foul. It can't put anyone in prison.

At 15: Who makes Myanmar obey? No one, directly. The court can't jail a general; it leans on the Security Council and other countries. Is a ruling nobody can enforce worth seven years in court? Second: six of fifteen judges voted no. Does the split weaken it, or is that what honest judging looks like?

Why this works at age 15: At fifteen they can hold two true things: the court's first finding of state genocide, and six judges who read the same evidence and voted no. Kuhn says argument is a practised skill, so the script asks whether the split is a flaw or honest judging. Expect 'is Israel next?' That case is pending and this ruling says nothing about it.
