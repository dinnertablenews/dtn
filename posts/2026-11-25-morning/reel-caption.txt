Freedom and Feathers, two four-month-old turkeys from a West Virginia farm, got a "complete and total pardon" on the White House South Lawn on Tuesday. They retire to Texas A&M. The ceremony is the 79th; the pardon itself dates to George H. W. Bush in 1989. Trump used his speech to talk up his new ballroom and complain about the election.

At age 7: Two turkeys named Freedom and Feathers went to the White House yesterday, and the president said nobody gets to eat them. Together they weigh 82 pounds, about as much as a big dog. Now they go to live at a college in Texas. Grown-ups play this joke every year before Thanksgiving, and the turkeys get the good end of it.

At 12: A pardon is the president wiping out someone's punishment. Since 1989, presidents have given one to a turkey each Thanksgiving, for fun. This year's two were picked in an online poll and will retire at Texas A&M. A tradition works like field day: it repeats, so nobody has to decide it again.

At 15: Why does a president pardon a turkey? The turkey industry has sent one since 1947, and Bush made it a pardon in 1989. Trump used Tuesday's to praise his new ballroom and complain about the election. Second: PETA says the pardoned birds usually die young anyway. Does that spoil the joke?

Why this works at age 15: A fifteen-year-old can hold two true things at once: a harmless 79-year-old tradition, and a president using its microphone for a ballroom and an election grievance. The script gives them the dates and PETA's line, then asks whether the joke survives. Expect 'why is this even news?' Fair question. The answer is the cameras.
