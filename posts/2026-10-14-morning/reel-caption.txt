At age 7: The president has a new helper whose job is to tell reporters what he is doing. Her name is Katie Zacharia. On her first day she stood at a podium for 14 minutes, shorter than recess. Her big news was that a lady who worked for the president in Greece has to stop, because someone says she asked for money she should not have. The grown-ups have it.

At 12: A newspaper says Guilfoyle, the ambassador to Greece, asked a donor to pay her $100,000 credit card bill and offered meetings with officials back. The donor said no. Tuesday Trump asked her to resign. A hall monitor can't sell hall passes, even if nobody buys.

At 15: Trump has stood by plenty of officials accused of using their office for gain. Why not this one? He said "I didn't like what I was reading." Second: the new press secretary says her job is promoting the president's message. Her first briefing took a handful of questions in 14 minutes. Who is a briefing for?

Why this works at age 12: This age is working out whether adults apply rules consistently, so the script runs the chain in order: a report, a refusal, a resignation. The hall pass is the rule underneath, that you cannot sell what the job gives you. Expect "but she didn't get the money." Asking is the problem, if she asked. She says she didn't.

The dinner table question: Would you drop a friend over a rule they say they didn't break? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
