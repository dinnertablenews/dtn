Up to a foot, until February.

Pulses of warm water called Kelvin waves are lifting the sea by up to a foot and moving north up California at about 6 mph. Oregon and Washington are next. They don't crash like a tsunami; the ocean sloshes like a bathtub. The waves run through February, and a super El Niño is behind them. California declared an emergency in September.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 7: The Pacific Ocean is sloshing like water in a bathtub, and a bulge of it is sliding up the coast of California. It lifts the sea about as high as a ruler is long. Nobody can see it, and boats don't bob on it. Beaches may get smaller this winter, and the grown-ups are piling up sand to be ready.

At 11: Winds along the equator usually push Pacific water west toward Asia. This El Niño they slackened, so the pile slid back east and up the coast. One bulge is moving up California at 6 mph, and alone it floods nothing. It's a step stool: every high tide and storm this winter starts a foot higher.

At 15: When a scientist says a wave "floods nothing on its own," is that reassuring or a warning? Kelvin waves are lifting the West Coast sea up to a foot through February, and the big El Niños set erosion records. Second: California declared an emergency before any storm. When is early too early?

Why this works at age 7: A six-year-old reasons from what they can touch, so the script measures the sea with a ruler and makes the ocean a bathtub. Cantor found this age fears what it can picture, so the wave gets no crash, only a slosh. Expect "will our house get wet?" Say the water is only taller where it meets the beach, and the grown-ups are watching it.
