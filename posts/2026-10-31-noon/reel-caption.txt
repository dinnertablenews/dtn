At age 6: A woman in Tennessee hurt someone very badly, long before you were born. As a punishment the state tried to end her life, and it did not work. A month later she says her arms and her throat still hurt, like the worst sore throat, and doctors at the prison where she lives are looking after her. It is far from us, and the grown-ups have it.

At 10: Tennessee gave Christa Pike two doses of a lethal drug on 30 September, and she lived. A month on she says she is in constant pain and blames incompetence. The AP says an IV line likely let the drug pool in her arm, like a hose slipping off the tap. The governor ordered a review.

At 15: If a state botches an execution, does it owe the prisoner a different sentence? Her lawyers say life in prison. The governor suspects it stands unless a court says otherwise. Pike blames incompetence for her pain, then says she thinks of Colleen Slemmer's family. Can both be true?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things at once, and this story asks for that: Pike tortured and killed a 19-year-old, and the state then hurt her in a way the governor is investigating. The script hands them the open question, who owes what now. Expect "Would you let them try again?" Answer straight, then argue the other side.
