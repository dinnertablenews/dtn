At age 6: In Kenya, a country in Africa, a few people have caught a very bad germ called Ebola. Doctors are keeping everyone who was near them in a special hospital so the germ can't jump to anyone else, like a lid on a pot. It is far from here, and the grown-ups there know what to do. Washing your hands does the same job at home.

At 10: A man with Ebola travelled from Congo to Nairobi and died on October 5. It passes by touching a sick person's blood or vomit, so everyone close to him went into quarantine. One relative, 65, got sick anyway and has died. Quarantine is a fence around whoever might be next: if one gets sick, it stops there.

At 15: If someone in quarantine dies anyway, did the quarantine fail? Kenya's second Ebola death was a woman of 65, isolated as a contact of the first patient, her relative. Nobody outside that cluster has caught it. Second: the US told Americans not to travel to Kenya at all. Nine cases, one cluster. Proportionate?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things: the woman died in quarantine, and the quarantine did its job. The script hands them that pair, then a judgement call, a travel warning for a whole country over nine cases in one cluster. Kuhn's point: arguing well is practised, and it needs an honest partner.
