At age 6: At a big college in New York, the teachers voted 75 to 50 that they don't trust the people in charge to keep students safe. Picture a class where most hands go up to say the rules aren't working. A grown-up student was hurt, and the grown-ups are arguing over who should have done more. You're safe, and the grown-ups have it.

At 11: A former Cornell student says she was raped at a fraternity party in 2024 and the university let her down. The school punished some of the men; professors wanted more. Friday they voted 75-50 that they don't trust the leaders to fix it. Like a class voting on the principal: a message, but the school board decides.

At 13: Should leaders lose their jobs over how they handled something they didn't do? Cornell's professors voted 75-50 that they don't trust the leaders on sexual assault; a former student says she was raped at a fraternity. Second: 50 voted no, some to wait for an outside review. Is waiting confidence?

Why this works at age 13: A thirteen-year-old can hold two true things, so the script gives both: 75 professors said the leaders failed, and 50 said no, some because an outside review isn't done. The second question sits on the 50: the harder side is the practice. Expect "were the men punished?" Two expelled, two suspended, two wrote papers. All seven deny it.
