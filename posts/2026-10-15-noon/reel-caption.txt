Social Security checks rise 3.6% in January, the biggest raise since 2023. For the average retired worker that is about $76 a month, just over $900 a year. It reaches 76 million people. The number is a formula: July-to-September prices against last year's. AARP polled 1,000 adults over 50, and 80% of recipients said that won't keep up.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 7: Every month the government sends money to people who have retired, like a lot of grandparents. In January those checks get bigger, about $76 more, because food and gas cost more than they did last year. It's like a step stool that grows when the shelf gets higher. Nothing changes for you.

At 12: Social Security pays 76 million retired and disabled people every month. Each fall the government compares July-to-September prices with last year's. Prices rose 3.6%, so every check rises 3.6% in January, about $76 for the average retiree. A scoreboard: prices move, checks move the same.

At 15: Is a 3.6% raise a raise? Checks rise 3.6% in January, matching prices. But people over 65 spend 2.5 times what workers do on health care, and the count follows workers. Four in five recipients told AARP it won't cover it. Second: the fund runs short in six years. Who covers the gap?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things at once: the raise matches prices, and most of the people getting it say it falls short, because the index counts a worker's shopping and retirees buy more health care. The script asks for both, then who pays when the fund runs short. Expect "will it be there for me?" The articles don't say. Say so.
