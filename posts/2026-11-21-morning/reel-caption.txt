At age 7: Russia is fighting a war with its neighbor Ukraine. On Friday two of its missiles flew too far and came down in Poland, the country next door, one of them in a forest. It's like a ball thrown so hard it lands in the neighbor's yard. Nobody was hurt, and the grown-ups who run Poland are already on it.

At 12: Russia fired over 110 missiles and 400 drones at Ukraine overnight. Two kept going and came down in Poland, 22 miles from European soldiers in training. Poland is in NATO: 32 countries sworn to defend each other, a buddy system with rules. All 32 must agree before anyone fights.

At 16: Is this how a bigger war starts? Article 5 says an attack on one NATO member is an attack on all 32, but only if all 32 agree it was one. Poland's prime minister calls it intentional. Britain's intelligence chief says Russia doesn't want war and fears a miscalculation. Who decides which this was?

Why this works at age 16: A sixteen-year-old can hold two true things at once: the missiles were probably deliberate, and Russia probably does not want war with NATO. The script hands them both and asks who decides, Kuhn’s point that argument is practised on real cases. Expect ‘so Article 5 is a bluff?’ No: a promise with a vote attached, by design.
