An unfair trial means do it over. Not go free.

A Manhattan judge sentenced Harvey Weinstein Wednesday to 15 years for sexually assaulting Miriam Haley, a TV production assistant, in 2006. A jury convicted him in 2020. New York's top court threw that out in 2024, and a second jury convicted him again. He has already served six years. Haley told the court: "It's a life sentence for me."

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 6: A man who used to make movies hurt a woman a long time ago, and yesterday a judge said he has to stay in prison for 15 years. The first trial got mixed up, so they did it over, the way you replay a point when the rules get muddled. He's been in prison six years already. The grown-ups have it.

At 11: In 2020 a jury found Harvey Weinstein guilty of assaulting Miriam Haley. In 2024 New York's top court threw it out: the jury had heard from women whose cases weren't on trial. Against the rules. So they replayed it, like a referee ordering a do-over, and a second jury said guilty. Wednesday: 15 years.

At 15: If a trial was unfair to the defendant, is the fix a new trial or freedom? New York's top court picked a new trial for Harvey Weinstein, and a second jury convicted him again. Then: Miriam Haley testified twice and called it "a life sentence for me." Is that a price the system should ask of her?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things: the first trial was unfair to Weinstein, and the second found him guilty. The script hands them both, then Haley's cost, so fairness has a person in it. Kuhn's point is that argument is practised, so let them argue for the retrial first. Expect "would you have testified again?" Answer honestly.
