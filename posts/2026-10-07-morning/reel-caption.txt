Wire a cubic kilometre of ice and wait for a flash.

Francis Halzen won the Nobel Prize in Physics on Tuesday for IceCube: a cubic kilometre of South Pole ice fitted with light sensors. It catches neutrinos, particles from distant galaxies that pass through the Earth, and through you, untouched. Most slip past the ice too. Once in a while one hits it and flashes. He pitched it in 1988.

Source: BBC News

At age 6: A scientist named Francis Halzen won a big prize for catching ghost particles at the South Pole. They come from space and zip through the whole Earth: billions go through your fingernail every second and you never feel one. His trap is a block of ice taller than any building, with lights inside watching for one tiny flash. They never bump a thing.

At 10: Neutrinos from far galaxies pass straight through the Earth. In 1988 Francis Halzen pitched a trap: a cubic kilometre of South Pole ice wired with light sensors. Now and then one hits the ice and flashes, and the flash points home. A porch light on a sensor: nothing all night, then one flash.

At 16: Would you give a Nobel to a man who calls what his machine will find "impossible to predict"? Francis Halzen pitched IceCube in 1988 and led the team that built it. Second: he says it needs many more telescopes to be real astronomy. Keep paying?

Why this works at age 10: This age is working out chains of cause and effect, and the script hands them one with four links: a particle nothing stops, clear ice wired with sensors, a flash when one hits, a direction read from it. The porch light is a mechanism they own. Expect "why ice?" The South Pole's is unusually clear, so the sensors see the flash.
