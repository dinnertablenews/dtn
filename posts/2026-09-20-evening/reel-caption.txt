Exit polls put Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats at 5% in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania on Sunday, the party's worst state result since World War II. The far-right AfD led there with 38%. In Berlin the CDU came second to the Left. Merz called it a disaster, said he will stay on, and dropped his trip to the UN next week.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 7: Germany picked new leaders for two of its states on Sunday, and the party of the man who runs the whole country got 5 votes out of every 100. That's one kid in a class of 20. He says he's staying in his job anyway. The grown-ups have it.

At 10: Germany's states elect their own parliaments, like states here electing governors. On Sunday Merz's party got 5% in one and second in Berlin, two weeks after losing a third to the far-right AfD. Three losses in a row, and the coach keeps the playbook: his reforms come anyway, he says.

At 13: Voters handed Merz's party 5%. Change course or hold it? He says the loss makes his reforms more urgent. Second: the AfD won a state with 38% and still won't govern, since no other party will work with it. Democracy working, or voters overruled?

Why this works at age 10: This age is working out cause and effect and whether adults change course when things go wrong, so the script hands them the chain: three state losses in a row, and a leader who keeps the playbook. Expect “shouldn't he quit?” Say some in his party think so, and eight state leaders said no. Let them make the call.

The dinner table question: After a big loss, do you change the plan or push harder? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
