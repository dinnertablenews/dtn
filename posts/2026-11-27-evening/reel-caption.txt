Mexico says Mexico's. The DEA is taking tips anyway.

On Wednesday the head of the US Drug Enforcement Administration asked people in Mexico to send anonymous WhatsApp tips about criminals and corrupt officials. On Friday President Claudia Sheinbaum filed a formal complaint with the US Embassy and called it interference. A Sinaloa journalist sent a tip the first day and got a quick reply.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 7: A police team from the United States put up a phone number and asked people in Mexico, the country south of us, to send secret tips about criminals. Mexico's president said no thanks, that's our job. It's like the neighbors putting a lost-and-found box in your yard. The two countries are arguing in letters, and the grown-ups have it.

At 8: On Wednesday the DEA, the US drug police, posted a WhatsApp number for anonymous tips on criminals and crooked officials in Mexico. By Friday Mexico's president had filed a complaint: that's Mexico's job. Like a rival coach handing your players a number to report fouls.

At 17: If your own government won't catch the crooks, is it wrong to call someone else's? A Sinaloa journalist sent the DEA a tip on day one, fed up with narco-governments. Sheinbaum calls it interference, days after asking the US for a drone. Where does help become meddling?

Why this works at age 8: A child this age is working out jurisdiction without the word for it: whose rules apply on whose ground. The chain is dated, a post Wednesday, tips Thursday, a complaint Friday, and the coach from another team makes the reach familiar. Expect 'but what if the tips are true?' That is the journalist's point. Sheinbaum says it is her job.

The dinner table question: If the neighbors spot trouble at your house, should they call it in? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
