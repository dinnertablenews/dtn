At age 6: The president wants a giant stone arch near Washington, taller than the Statue of Liberty. The city next door and the state across the river asked a judge to stop it. They say three years of building would jam the roads, like a traffic cone that never moves. A judge decides, and nothing changes for you.

At 11: Trump wants a 250-foot arch at the bridge to Arlington cemetery. Work could start next week. DC and Virginia sued Saturday: three years of building would jam the road and slow ambulances. They want a judge to pause it first, like a referee stopping play to check the call.

At 13: Who gets a say when the federal government builds on federal land in your city? DC and Virginia say nobody asked them, and sued. The White House says the arch honors veterans. Second: the judge asked what the rush was beyond "bedazzling the city." Is hurry a reason to stop someone?

Why this works at age 11: This age is working out rules and who enforces them, so the script runs the chain: a plan, two governments that object, a judge who can pause it. The referee is the enforcer they know. Expect "can a judge really stop the president?" She can pause the work while the case is heard, and she said she'd be quick.
