Years of waves and quakes. It went during a storm.

The Hōlei Sea Arch, 90 feet of lava rock at the end of Chain of Craters Road in Volcanoes National Park, has fallen into the Pacific. It was standing on 23 September and gone by 27 September. The park was shut for Tropical Storm Nolo, so nobody saw it go. The park service warned in 2021 that it would crumble one day.

Source: BBC News

At age 7: A huge rock arch in Hawaii, as tall as a nine-floor building, stood over the ocean for 550 years. Last week it fell into the sea. Waves had been nibbling at it for years, the way they flatten a sandcastle. Nobody was near it. The park was shut for a storm, and the lookout had been moved back long ago.

At 9: The arch was lava rock, 90 feet tall, and the ocean had worked on it for 550 years. Earthquakes in 2018 cracked the cliffs. In 2020 storm waves broke a chunk off the bottom, so the park moved the lookout back. Last weekend, in another storm, it fell. A loose tooth: wiggle, wiggle, gone.

At 14: In 2021 the park service called the arch "beautiful, but temporary" and said it would fall. It did, 550 years in, during a storm. Does knowing a place is on the clock change whether you go? Second: thousands posted photos of it within a day. Is a photo of something gone worth more, or less?

Why this works at age 9: This age is building chains of cause and effect: quakes in 2018, a chunk lost to waves in 2020, a storm weekend to finish it. The loose tooth is the mechanism: it loosens in steps and goes all at once. Expect "why didn't they fix it?" You cannot prop up a sea cliff. You move the people back. The park did.
