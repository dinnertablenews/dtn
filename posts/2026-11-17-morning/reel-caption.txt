At age 7: The man who ran the company that makes the fastest computer chips quit last week because he broke his company's rules. Lots of people, even the president, signed a letter asking for him back. The company's bosses said no. Like a team: the players can ask, but the coach picks the captain. The grown-ups have it.

At 11: Nvidia's boss quit after breaking the company's rules. 175,000 people signed a petition to bring him back, Elon Musk too, and Trump said he wouldn't mind. But the board hires and fires the boss. The petition is the crowd, the board is the referee, and the call stands.

At 17: Should a company take back a boss who broke its rules because he's the best at the job? Musk says Nvidia's deals were "a handshake with Jensen." A professor calls that the genius theory. Second: Trump holds Nvidia stock. Does that change what his opinion is worth?

Why this works at age 17: At seventeen they can hold two true things, Huang was brilliant at the job and broke its rules, and they can reason about what is not in the room: what a president's stock holding does to his opinion. The script hands them a professor's label to knock down. Expect "would you take him back?" Answer with a reason, then hear theirs.
