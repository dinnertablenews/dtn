At age 6: The grown-ups who make the country's rules work in one big room with 435 chairs. The last election gave one team, the Democrats, 258 of those chairs, the most for one team since 1992, long before you were born. Picture a tug of war with far more people on one end of the rope. Nothing changes at our house.

At 9: The House has 435 seats, and a bill needs 218 votes. Democrats won 258, so think of each vote as a race to 218: dozens of their runners can drop out and the rest still cross the line. Every bill still ends at Trump's desk, and he can veto it.

At 13: What is a mandate worth with a veto at the other end? Democrats won 258 House seats and 56 in the Senate, so bills pass and subpoenas go out, but Trump signs or vetoes every law. Rep. Tom Suozzi wants 75% of their time on prices, 25% on investigations. Second: is that the right split?

Why this works at age 13: A thirteen-year-old can hold a record majority and an unchanged veto in one thought, and Kuhn's point is that arguing from both is a skill that improves with practice. Suozzi's 75/25 split is a concrete claim to push on. Expect 'so what did the election change?' Who writes the bills and who gets subpoenaed. Not who signs them.
