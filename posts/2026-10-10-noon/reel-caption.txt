El Niño held the season back. This one came in at 110 mph.

Hurricane Isaias came ashore on Florida's Panhandle on Friday evening with 110 mph winds, a category 2. Florida, Alabama and Georgia declared emergencies. Forecasters warned of storm surge up to 9 feet. More than 350,000 homes in Florida and 170,000 in Alabama lost power. It is the Atlantic's first hurricane this year, the latest start in 30 years.

Source: BBC News

At age 6: A big storm called Hurricane Isaias hit Florida last night, with wind faster than a car on the highway. Most families drove away before it came. One man stayed and put his ducks and chickens in the bathroom. The storm gets weaker over land, and the grown-ups have it.

At 9: El Niño in the Pacific kept the Atlantic quiet until October, the latest start in 30 years. Then Isaias came ashore in Florida at 110 mph, moving fast enough to carry wind deep into Alabama and Georgia. A storm on land is a car with the engine cut: it coasts, then stops.

At 15: Why no Atlantic hurricane until October? The BBC says El Niño held the season back, then Isaias hit Florida at 110 mph. Second: a man in Navarre stayed while his street left. "We get them all the time." Who owns that risk, him or the governor?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things at once: one quiet season proves nothing, and a 110 mph storm in October is still a fact to reason from. The script adds a man who stayed against the advice and asks who owns that risk. Argument is practised, so let them argue his side first. Expect "is this climate change?" The articles name only El Niño.

The dinner table question: Would you stay in your house if everyone on your street left? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
