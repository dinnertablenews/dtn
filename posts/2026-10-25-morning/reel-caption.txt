Hundreds of thousands marched across France on Saturday, the second weekend of protests over school funding and the cost of living. The interior ministry counted 410,000; the unions said about 800,000. Most of the day was calm. Then came clashes at the end in Paris and other cities, nearly 1,000 arrests and two people seriously injured.

Source: BBC News

At age 6: In France, a country across the ocean, a huge crowd walked through the streets on Saturday. 410,000 people, enough to fill a big football stadium five times. They want more teachers in their schools, and they want food and petrol to cost less. A few fought with the police at the end. Most just walked and went home.

At 8: 410,000 marched across France on Saturday over short-staffed schools and rising prices. That is the government's count. The unions say 800,000. Nobody counts a crowd one by one. Each side guesses, like jellybeans in a jar, and the low guess was the government's. At the end, fireworks met tear gas in Paris.

At 15: Who gets to say what a protest was? A student in Lyon counted 150 of them, mostly students, before the tear gas came. The interior minister counted 500 rioters at the back of the Paris march. Second: 70,000 police were out and last week's looting didn't repeat. Peace kept, or a side picked?

Why this works at age 8: This age is working out whether grown-ups apply rules fairly, and two counts of one crowd is exactly that puzzle, so the script gives them the jar and lets them weigh who guessed and why. Expect "so who's lying?" Maybe nobody. A guess leans toward the guesser, and the honest move is to say both numbers out loud.

The dinner table question: Two sides count the same crowd. Whose number do you trust? Tell us what your kid said, and how old they are.
