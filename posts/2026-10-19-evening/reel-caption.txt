At age 7: A rocket left Japan this morning carrying a spacecraft to Mars. Next year it will reach a tiny moon called Phobos, shaped like a potato, and drop off a rover the size of a microwave. Later it will scoop up about two teaspoons of dust and fly them home. No people are on board, only robots, so nobody is far from home.

At 8: Phobos has almost no gravity, so a lander can't just set down: it could bounce off or sink. So Japan sends a rover first to crawl around for 100 days and map the ground. Only then does the lander come, for two hours, to drill up 10 grams. The kid who tests the ice before anyone skates.

At 16: Would you spend $578 million for two teaspoons of dust? Five agencies did, to learn whether Phobos is debris from a crash into Mars or a captured asteroid. Second: NASA has 30 sample tubes stuck on Mars with no ride home while Japan and China aim for 2031. Does being first matter?

Why this works at age 7: A seven-year-old reasons from what is in front of them, so the script hands over a potato-shaped moon and a microwave-sized rover and leaves the orbits alone. The one worry at this age is who is on the rocket, so the script answers it last, the line they keep. Expect "can I go to Mars?" Not yet. This dust is part of working out how.
