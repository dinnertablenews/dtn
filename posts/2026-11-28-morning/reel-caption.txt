At age 6: Scientists near Seattle are checking on 76 orcas, the big black-and-white whales, to see if they're healthy. When an orca breathes out, the spray is as wide as a basketball, and a little flying robot catches a bit of it. A dog named Eba sniffs the air from the boat to find whale poop. The whales don't mind; to them the drone is just a funny seagull.

At 8: The Southern Resident orcas eat Chinook salmon. A century of dams and overfishing shrank those salmon from over 100 pounds to about 12. Hungry whales lose calves. So scientists catch each whale's spout with a drone and its droppings with a dog, and build it a health file like your doctor's.

At 16: Why do the orcas that eat seals thrive while the salmon-eaters die out in the same water? Bigg's orcas calve every two years; the Southern Residents miscarry, and their salmon have shrunk from over 100 pounds to about 12. Second: Deborah Giles says the job is to "turn back the clock." Why this works at age 16: A teenager can hold two true things at once: the drone checkups are good work, and 76 whales with miscarrying mothers means the work is losing.
