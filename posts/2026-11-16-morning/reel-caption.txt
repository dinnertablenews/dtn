At age 7: A company called Nvidia makes the fastest computer chips in the world, the tiny brains inside a computer. America has a rule: don't sell them to China. A company in Singapore bought more of them than anyone nearby, and people think it let Chinese companies use them anyway. Lawyers are checking now, and the grown-ups have it.

At 10: Nvidia can't sell its best chips to China. Megaspeed, in Singapore, bought more than anyone nearby. The suspicion: Chinese companies got at them through a back door. Think of a hall pass: who borrowed one? It says it followed the rules and hired lawyers to check.

At 14: Can a company investigate itself? Megaspeed hired its own lawyers after Singapore, Congress and the US Treasury secretary pointed at it. Second: Nvidia's boss resigned over affairs with a woman the US suspects, but hasn't shown, is a spy. Should suspicion cost a job?

Why this works at age 14: A fourteen-year-old can hold two true things: a company's own lawyers are not a neutral referee, and nobody has shown the chips reached China or that she was a spy. Kuhn found arguing is a practised skill, so the second question is a case still open: what counts as enough? Expect "was she a spy?" Justice does not know yet.
