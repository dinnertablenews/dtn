It had to brake the whole way. The sun pulls hard.

Two linked spacecraft, one European and one Japanese, slipped into orbit around Mercury on Saturday. The $2 billion BepiColombo mission launched in 2018 and looped past Earth, Venus and Mercury nine times to slow down, logging more than 6 billion miles. It is only the third visitor to the sun's closest planet, and the first since 2015.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 6: Two robot spaceships just reached Mercury, the planet closest to the sun. They flew for eight years to get there, longer than you have been alive, and Mercury is only a bit bigger than our moon. In the daytime it gets hotter than the oven at home. Nobody is on board, just cameras and tools, and the grown-ups who built it have it.

At 9: Mercury sits next to the sun, and the sun's gravity pulls everything in. Fly straight at it and you arrive too fast to stop, so BepiColombo spent eight years looping past Earth, Venus and Mercury, nine fly-bys, each one a squeeze of the bike brake. On Saturday Mercury's weak gravity finally caught it.

At 14: Why so few visits to the planet next door? Only two craft had been. Europe and Japan spent $2 billion because Mercury makes no sense: a huge metal core in a body barely bigger than our moon, ice in craters on an 800-degree world. Second: is 'we don't know why' worth $2 billion?

Why this works at age 9: At eleven they want the cause behind the number, and eight years is a number begging for one. The script hands them the chain: the sun pulls, a direct flight arrives too fast, so nine fly-bys bleed off speed like a bike brake on a long hill. Expect 'what if the brake had failed?' The BBC's answer: it shoots past Mercury. No second try.
