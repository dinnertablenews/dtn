Crystalyne Curley won the Navajo Nation's presidency Tuesday, the first woman to hold it since the tribal government was set up a century ago. The same night Peggy Flanagan became the first Native American woman elected to the US Senate, and Deb Haaland the first to be a governor, in New Mexico. The Seneca Nation picked a woman president too.

Source: PBS NewsHour

At age 7: A woman named Crystalyne Curley was just picked to lead the Navajo Nation, whose land spreads across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. In third grade she went on a field trip, watched the leaders argue, and decided that was where she wanted to be. No woman had ever had the job. Now one does.

At 10: The Navajo Nation's government was set up a century ago, and every president since has been a man. Some Navajo people say tradition means the top job is for men. Crystalyne Curley ran anyway and won, narrowly. Think of a club rule nobody ever wrote down, until someone asks why.

At 15: Only four Native Americans have been US senators in 230 years, all men. Why? Peggy Flanagan is now the first Native woman senator, Deb Haaland the first Native woman governor, and Crystalyne Curley the first woman to lead the Navajo Nation. Second: is a first a win, or a measure of the wait?

Why this works at age 15: A teenager can hold two true things at once: three firsts in one night, and a Senate that seated four Native people in 230 years, all men. The script sets the win against the wait and asks which is the story. Expect "why does it matter what they are?" Curley's answer: a higher standard, and twice the work.
